India has 146 million farming households. Each one is a small enterprise — carrying the weight of feeding a billion people, absorbing the volatility of weather, markets, and policy. And yet, when we talk about a "farming crisis," we reach for statistics. We talk about MSP, loan waivers, and input costs. We rarely sit with what it actually means when a farmer decides to stop.

"The soil remembers what we choose to forget. When we drain it of nutrients for yield, we drain ourselves of nourishment for generations."

— ANAAD Field Journal, Rabi Season 2024

The Letter That Was Never Sent

In the winter of 2023, we visited a family in Sikar district, Rajasthan. The patriarch — a third-generation wheat farmer — showed us a piece of paper he had drafted on his phone's notepad. It was a resignation letter. Not to an employer. To farming itself. He never sent it. But it existed. That it existed at all told us everything we needed to know about the state of Indian agriculture in the era of synthetic inputs.

His father had grown the same Sona Moti wheat variety for forty years without purchasing a single bag of fertilizer. The soil was fed by cows. The pests were managed by companion planting. The yield was enough. Then the Green Revolution arrived — and with it, the promise of more. More yield. More income. More security.

What "More" Cost Us

Nutrient Loss 30% Decline in Zinc & Iron in modern wheat vs. heirloom varieties (ICAR, 2024)
Farmer Debt ₹74,121 Average per-household agricultural debt in India (NSSO, 2023)
Input Cost Rise 340% Increase in synthetic fertilizer costs over the last two decades

The "more" cost the farmer his independence. Hybrid seeds required repurchase every season. Synthetic fertilizers required cash upfront. Pesticides became non-negotiable once the natural soil ecosystem collapsed. Each cycle pushed the farmer deeper into a debt trap — one engineered not by laziness or poor decisions, but by a system designed to extract maximum yield from the land without regard for its long-term health.

The Soil Cannot Resign

Here is the part that keeps our team awake: the farmer can write a resignation letter. The soil cannot. Once you strip a piece of land of its microbial life, its carbon content, its mineral balance — it does not simply bounce back when you change your mind. The damage is measured in decades. Sometimes in generations.

Wheat fields in Sikar, Rajasthan — where heirloom varieties are still grown using ICBN methods. Photo: ANAAD Archives.

ICBN as a Counter-Resignation

Indian Cow Based Natural farming — ICBN — is not romantic nostalgia. It is a precisely documented, peer-reviewed agricultural methodology that restores soil health through the biological inputs of the desi cow: Jeevamrit, Panchagavya, Gomaya compost, and Gomutra Ark. Our farms in Gannaur, Haryana have been transitioning partner farmers to ICBN for three years. What we have observed is not a miracle. It is a restoration.

The resignation letter is still on that farmer's phone in Sikar. He has not deleted it. But this past Rabi season, he chose not to send it. He chose, instead, to accept ANAAD's ICBN seed and support — and to grow his family's Sona Moti wheat one more time. That choice is not guaranteed to last. But it is, right now, a beginning.

"Every family that chooses to source their grain honestly is writing a counter-letter. Not to farming. To the system that tried to end it."

What You Can Do

The revolution starts on your plate — not in Parliament, not in policy documents. When you choose to understand where your grain comes from, who grew it, and under what conditions, you are participating in something larger than a grocery transaction. You are making a land decision. A farmer decision. A soil decision.

Read the batch certificate next time you order from ANAAD. Understand that the SGS lab result on the back of your flour pack is not a marketing asset. It is proof that a farmer somewhere chose not to write that letter.
